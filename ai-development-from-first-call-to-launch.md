# AI development for business: from first call to launch

![Cover](https://dxggowrfyirnanabowhr.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/site-images/site-ai-development-from-first-call-to-launch-1791594709.jpg)

Any AI system I ship travels the same road: five stages, always in the same order, whether the client is a clinic booking bot or a factory analytics dashboard. People usually expect the road to run through code. It barely does - the coding stretch is the shortest leg of the trip, roughly a fifth of it. Most kilometres are driven before and after

## Stop one: the talk

Clients sometimes expect a demo on day one; what they get instead is questions - and the better the questions, the cheaper the project turns out. What hurts? Whose workflow stalls? Is this something a model can carry at all, and am I the right person to carry it? The brainstorm ends with a sentence everyone agrees on - the named problem. Skip that sentence and the smartest model downstream will happily solve something nobody asked for

## Stop two: the numbers

Next comes the part I enjoy most: drowning in the client's metrics until the bottleneck confesses. What arrives into the process, what leaves it, where the queue grows. This produces the blueprint - and a verdict people rarely want to hear early, though it saves months: whether the accumulated data is even enough for intelligence to function. Better a hard verdict at the blueprint than a silent hallucination in production

![Architect working on blueprints under a desk lamp](https://images.pexels.com/photos/9617369/pexels-photo-9617369.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)

## Stop three: the build

Now the machines get loud. By clock time this is the briefest stretch - a fifth or less of the whole, since the two earlier stops consumed about seventy percent - yet everything the client touches physically is built here: components wired together, the system wired into the workflow, tests run, training loops fed. Speed at this stop is never heroics. It is interest paid on preparation

![Two developers coding in a modern office](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7988079/pexels-photo-7988079.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&h=650&w=940)

## Stop four: after the applause

Handover day feels like curtains. It is actually intermission. The freshly deployed system immediately meets conversations and edge cases the tests never dreamt of, and the first weeks decide whether small quirks stay small. A delivered product nobody watches turns its owner into a full-time debugger of someone else's unfinished work. That is why support and growth closes the loop instead of decorating the invoice

Why keep the strict order? Because the costly failures cluster at the edges, never in the middle. A vague brief at stop one, a skipped data verdict at stop two - each burns more cash than a slow sprint ever will, and an abandoned stop four mails every bug straight to the client's inbox
